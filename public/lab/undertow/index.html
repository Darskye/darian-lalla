Undertow

The ocean has weather too. Satellites measure it from space, and a machine learns to forecast it.

Real data from NOAA · Free and open

About

What you're seeing

Every thread of light is a tracer carried by real measured or forecast flow. Tracers run tens of thousands of times faster than the water or air so that a day's pattern shows up in about a second. Each room's insights give the exact time-lapse factor.

Ocean

Radar altimeters on satellites measure the height of the sea surface to within a few centimetres. Warm eddies bulge up and cold ones dip. Because the Earth rotates, water flows along those slopes rather than down them, so the shape of the surface gives the current. NOAA turns it into daily current maps on a 25 km grid. Temperature comes from NOAA's daily OISST analysis, which blends satellites, ships and buoys. "Vs normal" compares each day with its 1991–2020 average.

Eddies are found automatically wherever spin beats stretching (the Okubo–Weiss test), then followed from day to day. Their size is the radius of the fastest swirling ring.

The model

A Fourier neural operator. It rewrites the last three days of currents as waves, keeps the largest ones, mixes them with weights it learned, and turns the result back into tomorrow's map. Feeding each forecast back in gives 14 days. It was trained on 2019–2024, tuned on 2025 and graded only on 2026, which it never saw.

Wind

Surface wind (10 m), the jet stream (250 hPa, about 10 km up), air temperature, rain and pressure from one run of NOAA's Global Forecast System, every 6 hours for 72 hours. The globe's shadow shows where it is night at that hour.
